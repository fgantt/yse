1. What the network does

An engine does two things over and over. It searches: it tries moves, the replies to them, the replies to those. And it evaluates: at the end of each line it looks at the position and gives it one number, plus for good and minus for bad from the side to move's view.

The search may visit millions of positions for a single move, and it can only be as good as the scores it gets back. The network is the evaluator. Older engines used hand-written rules ("a rook is worth this much"). A network learns its judgement from examples instead.

Technically the network is a formula with adjustable numbers in it, called weights: dials on a mixing desk. The formula's shape is fixed (section 7). Only the dial settings change. With random settings the scores are nonsense. Training is the search for dial settings whose scores are good. Ours has about 32 million dials, and its file, nn.bin, is 64 MB.

2. Learning from a teacher

"Good" needs a definition. We gather a very large number of examples, each made of a position and a target: the score we wish the network had given. The targets come from a teacher, a strong engine searching each position fairly deeply (YaneuraOu at depth 8 or 9 in our data).

So the network learns to predict, at a glance, what a deep search would conclude. Inside our search, that glance happens at every line's end, so the search plays as if it saw further than it does.

We also keep each game's result. The teacher's score says "this looks good". The result says "and the side that had it went on to win". The target blends them: 70% teacher, 30% result.

3. Measuring "wrong": the loss

For each example we compare the network's score with the target. Raw scores are a poor yardstick. A position the network calls +3000 and the teacher +2500 is won either way, so that error should hardly count. An error between −200 and +200 decides games. So both scores first pass through an S-shaped curve, the sigmoid, which turns a score into something like a winning chance between 0 and 1.

The error for one example is (network's chance − target's chance)², averaged over a batch of positions. That average is the loss: one number saying how wrong the network currently is. Training means pushing it down.

4. Turning the dials

With 32 million dials, trial and error is hopeless. Calculus answers a better question for every dial at once: "if I turn this one up a hair, does the loss rise or fall, and how fast?" Those answers together are the gradient. Computing them back through every layer is called backpropagation; PyTorch does it for us. One training step:

Learning rate

How far the dials move per step. Too big and they overshoot and training breaks: in our sweep, plain SGD at rates 3 and 30 collapsed to a network that says the same thing about every position. Too small and nothing is learned.

Optimizer: Adam or SGD

The rule for turning the dials. SGD moves each dial in proportion to its gradient. Adam, our default, keeps a running average of each dial's recent gradients and how much they vary, then moves steady dials confidently and noisy ones cautiously. In our pilots Adam clearly beat SGD.

Epochs and the schedule

We cut a run into epochs of 300 million positions. After each one we check the network, save a file, and multiply the learning rate by a factor (0.93 in h4). The run starts with big steps, to get roughly right fast, and ends with tiny ones, to settle precisely.

5. Held-out data, and why loss isn't strength

A network can get good at the exact positions it was shown without getting good at shogi, like a student who memorises past exam answers. This is overfitting. To catch it, a slice of the data is never trained on. After each epoch we measure the loss there (val loss), and how closely the network's scores follow the teacher's (val pearson, 1.0 = perfect).

Even the held-out loss only measures agreement with the teacher, one position at a time. Strength means winning games inside a search. The two rise together at first and then part ways, so every candidate network is judged by playing games.

6. Why data matters

The network can only learn what its examples show. More positions, more varied positions and better teachers (deeper search, stronger engine) all help, with diminishing returns:

  • Each new kind of position (sharp attacks, odd material, long endgames) teaches something.
  • More of what is already well covered teaches little.
  • The teacher's mistakes are learned along with its knowledge.

7. Inside our network

We train in the community's standard layout, YaneuraOu's HalfKP 256x2-32-32, saved as nn.bin. That way one file runs in our engine and in YaneuraOu. We implement the format with our own code. "NNUE" stands for efficiently updatable neural network, after the trick shown below.

What the network sees

Not a picture of the board, but a list of yes/no features, each one a combination like "my king on 5i, their silver on 4c". Pieces in hand count too. With 81 king squares × 1,548 piece-and-square cases there are 125,388 possible features, and in any position about 38 are on. Tying every piece to the king's square is what lets the network learn king safety. It reads the position twice, once from each king's side.

Why it is fast

Because the inputs are yes/no, the first layer's 256 numbers for a position are just the sum of the rows of the features that are on. A move changes only 2 to 4 features, so the engine updates the sum instead of recomputing it:

Decimals in training, integers in the engine

The engine computes with small integers for speed. Training needs smooth decimal numbers, because the dials move by tiny amounts. So the trainer works in decimals and rounds onto the format's integers each time it saves a network. Rounding costs a little accuracy, which the trainer reports as |int - float|: about 18–24 points on average in h4. Training with the rounding simulated is one of our open ideas.

The factoriser

A feature such as "my king on 1a, their pawn on 9i" may be rare in the data. To help rare features learn, the trainer adds one shared row per piece-and-square that ignores the king square. When the file is saved, that row is folded into every king square's row, so the file format is unchanged.

We use only data we made or data published under licences that allow it. No other engine's code, constants or networks are copied.

2 · Pack it: nnue_pack

The trainer reads one simple format of fixed 100-byte records: board, pieces in hand, side to move, teacher score, game result. nnue_pack (src/bin/nnue_pack.rs) converts our data and the public sets into it:

# our labelled games (JSONL) -> train.bin (shuffled) + val.bin (every 20th game held out)
cargo run --release --bin nnue_pack -- pack --corpus <corpus.jsonl> --out-dir <packed_dir> [--quiet]

# public sets in the 40-byte PackedSfenValue format (our own decoder); the last input is held out
cargo run --release --bin nnue_pack -- pack-psv --inputs <f1> <f2> ... --out-dir <packed_dir> [--quiet]

# sanity check a public file: decode a sample, check every stored move is legal
cargo run --release --bin nnue_pack -- psv-check --input <file>

--quiet drops positions that are in check or in the middle of a capture. A score taken mid-exchange is misleading to learn from, and on our own data this filter was worth +64 Elo. On the PC, the Windows build needs a relative output folder: it reads /mnt/c/… as C:\mnt\c\….

3 · Make the feature tables

The trainer must turn a board into exactly the features the engine uses. Rather than rewrite that logic in Python and risk a mismatch, nnue_pack writes lookup tables from the engine's own functions:

cargo run --release --features yo-diagnostic --bin nnue_pack -- tables --halfkp --out trainer/tables_halfkp.json

4 · Train: trainer/train.py

PyTorch, on the PC's GPU (~187,000 positions a second), Apple's GPU or a CPU. Setup is in trainer/README.md. Run h4 as an example:

cd trainer
python train.py --arch halfkp --tables tables_halfkp.json --header-from suisho5_nn.bin \
    --data ours,ours,ours,hao_s50q,hao_s150q,hao_s200bq,packed_s5,packed_s5_p1,packed_s5_p2,packed_s5_p3,packed_s5_p4 \
    --out runs/h4 --epochs 48 --epoch-size 300000000 --lr 1e-3 --gamma 0.93 \
    --sigmoid-scale 600 --clamp 3000 [--resume]
OptionWhat it does
--arch halfkptrain the standard layout (the default is our older a1-style network)
--header-fromany HalfKP 256x2-32-32 nn.bin. Only its header (format version, layout name, checksums) is reused so engines accept our file; none of its weights
--data a,b,cpacked folders. Batches are drawn from each in proportion to its size, so listing our small folder three times triples its share
--epochs, --epoch-size48 × 300M positions = 14.4B seen
--lr, --gammastarting learning rate; multiplied by gamma after each epoch
--optimizer sgd, --momentum, --plateau NSGD instead of Adam; or halve the rate when held-out loss stalls for N epochs
--sigmoid-scale, --clamp, --outcome-weightthe target and loss of sections 2–3 (600, 3000, 0.3)
--resumecontinue from runs/x/last.pt after a stop
--warm runs/y/last.ptstart from another run's network with a fresh optimizer and schedule (fine-tuning)
--batch, --val-n, --seed16,384 positions per step; 1M held-out positions; random seed

5 · Run it on the PC

Long runs use the Windows PC's GPU, started from the Mac. The run scripts live in matches/s48/, which is local and not in git. s48_pc_h4.sh runs the trainer with --resume, and hold_h4.sh keeps the SSH session open and reconnects if it drops, because detached jobs on the PC stall. A marker file stops a run. Every run gets a new folder.

6 · Check it

  1. Is the file read correctly? std_nnue_check scores positions with our engine's fast loader and with an independent, from-scratch reading of the format, and requires identical results, including during a search with incremental updates. 
    cargo run --release --features yo-diagnostic --bin std_nnue_check -- --net epoch_040.bin --corpus <corpus.jsonl>
  2. Quick strength checks. Every 4th epoch, per_epoch_h4.sh fetches the file and plays two matches, 120 games each:
    • ourmatch.py: our engine with the new network against our engine with a1 (the network we ship), 10k nodes per move.
    • netmatch.py: YaneuraOu with the new network against YaneuraOu with Suisho5's, 100k nodes per move.
    A fixed node budget makes results independent of machine load. 120 games resolve only about ±60 Elo, so choosing a final network takes a few hundred games.
  3. The ship gate. Before a network replaces a1: a sequential match (SPRT) at 1 second a move with 4 threads, as real games are played, which stops once the evidence is clear.

7 · Use it

Our engine: set the USI option NNUEFile to the file's path. A standard-format file is recognised and converted on load. NNUEScale (percent, default 100) scales its scores. YaneuraOu and other engines that read the format: put the file in a folder as nn.bin and point EvalDir at that folder.

  • Our own games matter. h1 without them stalled far below a1. Mixing them in was the first big gain.
  • Held-out loss keeps falling after strength stops rising. Choose networks by games.
  • This recipe is near its limit for this network size. A published reference, the same layout trained on 8B Suisho5 positions, reached −56 against Suisho5. h4 is at about −60.
  • A network alone moves our engine only so far. In our engine, a1 is level with YaneuraOu's older halfkpvm network (+3 ± 33), and Suisho5's own network beat halfkpvm there by only +35 at 10k nodes. A check now queued measures Suisho5's network and h4 against a1 at 10k, 100k and 1M nodes.
Next levers for the network: our own data, made with the stronger network at a deeper search (the loop of section 6); training that simulates the rounding; the remaining 3B Suisho5 positions; perhaps a larger standard layout. For playing strength in our engine, the search is the bigger lever.

10. Glossary

accumulator
the first layer's 256 numbers per king's view, updated as moves are made
Adam, SGD
rules for turning the weights at each step
batch
the positions used for one step (16,384)
bias
a constant added inside a layer, learned like a weight
clipping
limiting a layer's outputs to a range (0–127 in the engine)
epoch
here, a checkpoint every 300M positions: check, save a file, lower the learning rate
feature
a yes/no input: (king square, piece, square)
gradient
for each weight, which way and how fast the loss changes if it moves
held-out set
positions never trained on, used to catch memorising
learning rate
how far the weights move per step
loss
the average squared error in winning-chance terms; training pushes it down
NNUE
efficiently updatable neural network (section 7)
overfitting
learning the training examples rather than the game
pearson
how closely the network's scores follow the teacher's (1.0 = perfectly)
quiet filter
dropping positions in check or mid-capture before training
sigmoid
the S-curve that turns a score into a winning chance
SPRT
a match that stops as soon as its result is statistically clear
teacher
the deep search whose scores the network learns to predict
weights
the adjustable numbers of the network
